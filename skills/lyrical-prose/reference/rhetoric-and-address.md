# Rhetoric and address

Persuasive and relational devices, how the prose turns to the reader and carries conviction without raising its voice.

## Devices

- **Rhetorical question, then answer.** Pose a short question and unfold the answer in a longer breath. This both sets a thought in motion and gives it rhythm.
  - Anchor from chapter 3, "The river has its boundaries, its banks, but is a river all banks? or are the banks the final facts about the river?"
- **Direct address.** Turn to the reader, or to the world, to close the distance.
  - Anchor from chapter 2, "you can go on burdening your memory with such facts and never come to an end. But once you get hold of the law of gravitation you can dispense with the necessity of collecting facts…"
- **First-person meditation as structure.** Use "I" to stage a discovery, not to confess. The self is a vantage point, not the subject.
  - Anchor from chapter 4, "At one pole of my being I am one with stocks and stones."
- **Quote-and-gloss (optional).** Offer a short borrowed line, then interpret it in lived terms. Keep it rare, and never religion-specific.

## Do / Don't

- **Do** ask a question when you want the reader to lean in, then answer it generously.
- **Do** address the reader directly at a turning point to close the distance.
- **Do** use "I" to realise a thought in the open, as a shared act.
- **Don't** pile up rhetorical questions. One well-placed question carries more than a volley.
- **Don't** let first-person turn into confession or autobiography. The vantage is personal, the subject is not.
- **Don't** import scripture or a religious frame to borrow gravity a secular subject did not ask for.
