# Imagery and metaphor

How ideas are made visible. Every abstraction is tied to a plain, sensory thing the reader can picture. The images below are evidence of *technique*, a required *method*, not a required *vocabulary*. Draw fresh images from the writer's own subject.

## The method

- **Ground each abstraction in one plain image.** Prefer things from nature and common life, a seed and a tree, a wave, a river, a lamp, a road, a rainy night.
  - Anchor from chapter 2, "a mere fact is like a blind lane… But a truth opens up a whole horizon."
  - Anchor from chapter 3, "it does not blacken existence, just as the sky does not leave its stain upon the wings of the bird."
- **Let one image carry a whole paragraph.** Extend a single figure rather than piling several.
  - Anchor from chapter 3, "When we take a pitcherful of water from the sea it has its weight, but when we take a dip into the sea itself a thousand pitchersful of water flow above our head, and we do not feel their weight."
- **Use the plain image to make a precise distinction**, not merely to decorate.
  - Anchor from chapter 3, "the depth of a lake is judged not by the size of its cavity but by the depth of its water."
- **Personify abstractions** to give a thought agency and motion.
  - Anchor from chapter 4, "the universal is ever seeking its consummation in the unique."

## Do / Don't

- **Do** convert each abstract claim into something seen, heard, or touched.
- **Do** reach for nature and daily life first, because these carry the register.
- **Do** sustain one image to its end before moving to the next.
- **Don't** stack image upon image until they clog the idea. Crowding blurs the picture.
- **Don't** use mechanical, corporate, or digital metaphors, such as gears, pipelines, bandwidth, or engines of growth, which break the register at once.
- **Don't** let an image contradict the thought it serves. The picture must prove the point, not fight it.
